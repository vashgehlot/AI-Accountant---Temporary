# Nature, not pounds, decides what firms ask

No UK standard, professional body or HMRC manual sets a pound figure for when an accountant should code a transaction themselves and when they should query the client. The compilation standard ISRS 4410 uses the definition from the accounting frameworks: an item is material if it could influence users' decisions "by size or nature". It gives no percentages, and ICAEW's quality standard requires risk-based review without prescribing a threshold. In practice, a manager or partner sets limits for each client. Capitalisation floors commonly sit between £250 and £1,000, and prepayment floors between £100 and £1,000. These are loosely anchored on audit rules of thumb such as 5% of profit, recorded as accounting policies and applied by the bookkeeper. Alongside the limits sits a set of triggers that force a question whatever the amount, because the HMRC rules that matter depend on facts, not size. Those triggers are who attended a meal, whether there is a valid VAT invoice, whether a cost is personal or the director's, whether a receipt of money is unidentified, and who received a gift. Audit materiality is a published method: a benchmark times a percentage, with a lower "performance" level and a "clearly trivial" floor. Preparers borrow it informally, never formally. Mainstream software follows the same split. Vendors provide the rule types and leave the amounts blank for customers to fill in. AI bookkeepers use confidence scores to decide between posting and sending an item to the accountant, and asking the client is a separate, batched step. No product found documents a materiality setting or a review queue ranked by money at stake, which leaves a real gap in the market. For the app, this points to replacing the fixed £20, £50, £100/£500 and £300 limits and the "always ask about Sundry" rule with four things. The first is materiality worked out for each client from its own figures, which the firm can edit. The second is a set of nature flags that override size. The third is a test of whether the client's answer could change the VAT, the tax or the accounts. The fourth is tracking of running totals plus random sampling, so that small items cannot quietly add up.

## No rulebook sets a pound figure; firms set one per client

The formal duty to query comes from ISRS 4410 (Revised). A compilation "does not require the practitioner to verify the accuracy or completeness" of the client's information (para 6). The practitioner must still read the compiled accounts "in light of the practitioner's understanding of the entity's business" (para 31). If the records are "incomplete, inaccurate or otherwise unsatisfactory", the practitioner must raise this with management and ask for corrections, and withdraw if they are not provided (paras 32–33) ([ISRS 4410](https://www.ifac.org/system/files/publications/files/B012%202012%20IAASB%20Handbook%20ISRS%204410%20(revised).pdf)). Materiality is judged "in the context of the applicable financial reporting framework". Misstatements matter if "individually or in the aggregate" they could influence users. **No percentage or numeric benchmark appears anywhere in the standard** (A48, A50) ([ISRS 4410](https://www.ifac.org/system/files/publications/files/B012%202012%20IAASB%20Handbook%20ISRS%204410%20(revised).pdf)). HMRC's Business Income Manual treats materiality as an accounting concept. It quotes the FRS 102 test and warns that immaterial departures must not be used to achieve a particular presentation ([HMRC BIM31045](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim31045)). The ICAEW guidance written for UK compilations, TECH 07/16 AAF, is members-only, and its wording on materiality could not be checked ([ICAEW TECH 07/16 AAF](https://www.icaew.com/-/media/corporate/files/technical/technical-releases/audit/tech-0716-aaf-compilation-reports-incorporated.ashx)). No AAT materiality guidance for bookkeepers was found.

Firm quality control is equally non-numeric. ICAEW Practice Assurance Standard 4 asks smaller firms to review work before it is finalised and to "formulate your policy for reviews on a risk basis". It also asks them to cold-review a sample of files, which is essential for high-risk work, and to check that accounts are "materially correct". It never says what materially correct means in pounds ([ICAEW PA Standard 4](https://www.icaew.com/regulation/practice-assurance/practice-assurance-standards-smaller-firms/standard-4-smaller)). Practitioners on AccountingWEB call materiality "a basic tenet of accounts preparation", judged as "material for their size" ([AccountingWEB](https://www.accountingweb.co.uk/node/205091)). Where they want a number, they borrow audit conventions such as 5% of profit before tax, 0.5–1% of revenue or 1–2% of assets ([CPA Ireland](https://www.cpaireland.ie/CPAIreland/media/Education-Training/Study%20Support%20Resources/P1%20Auditing/Relevant%20Articles/materiality-and-its-practicalities.pdf); [OpenTuition](https://opentuition.com/topic/calculation-of-materiality/)). The forum evidence comes from search summaries of threads that could not be fetched in full, so it reflects practitioner opinion rather than survey data.

The area-specific limits the app hard-codes are, in practice, **client-specific policies, not industry constants**. On capitalisation, forum practitioners quote a "£200 rule of thumb" that is not applied rigidly, £250 with an asset register, and £1,000. One never capitalises below £3,000. Practitioners also note that a small business with few purchases should capitalise most items, such as computers ([AccountingWEB: Lower limit for capitalisation](https://www.accountingweb.co.uk/any-answers/lower-limit-for-capitalisation); [AccountingWEB node/216104](https://www.accountingweb.co.uk/node/216104)). Charities typically use £500–£1,000 ([ExpensePlus](https://expenseplus.co.uk/fund-accounting/?p=3647)). Prepayment floors range from about £100 for small businesses to £1,000 a month at larger scale. The test is whether leaving the prepayment out would change a user's view, and some practitioners say small-business prepayments "tend to be a whole lot of nothing" ([AccountingWEB: prepaid expense threshold](https://www.accountingweb.co.uk/any-answers/threshold-for-categorising-a-prepaid-expense-0); [AccountingWEB: Prepayments and Accruals](https://www.accountingweb.co.uk/any-answers/prepayments-and-accruals)). The orthodox advice is that the de minimis, "whatever it is", should apply for tax too ([AccountingWEB node/89428](https://www.accountingweb.co.uk/node/89428)). An accounting policy choice must also be applied consistently ([ICAEW TAS helpsheet](https://www.icaew.com/technical/tas-helpsheets/financial-reporting/can-i-capitalise-website-development-costs-under-frs-102)). The firm's £100 and £500 capitalisation limits and £300 prepayment limit therefore sit inside the normal range. What makes them unusual is that they are fixed for every client.

Query handling follows a recognisable three-layer pattern. First, the preparer resolves anything that can be inferred from payee history, the bank narrative or attached documents. Next, unresolved items go onto a batched list, monthly for bookkeeping clients or once at year end. Each item carries its date, amount, narrative and a proposed treatment, so the client can confirm in a word. Last, only material or sensitive items hold up completion. The rest get a judgement call recorded on file. One UK service tells clients to contact the firm about unusual or one-off transactions, new overseas suppliers, corrections to periods already filed for VAT, and any HMRC letter. Its rule is "If in doubt, ask", with no monetary thresholds ([1st Formations](https://help.1stformations.co.uk/accounting-service/when-to-contact-us-vs-when-to-handle-it-yourself)). One anecdotal example cut 40 first-pass flags to 22 real queries once false alarms were removed ([Sagnik Bhattacharya](https://sagnikbhattacharya.com/blog/bookkeeping-tax-year-end-handoff-ai)). Practitioners writing for AAT list what goes wrong on the client side. Clients answer only the first question, decide for themselves what information is needed, reveal personally paid expenses late, and send 14 emails in 30 minutes ([AAT Comment](https://www.aatcomment.org.uk/?p=57835)). A proposed answer on each query and a single batched list address most of those complaints directly. Suspense is expected to be empty by year end. A suspense credit hiding unidentified income understates profit, and balances should be cleared by journal once the cause is understood rather than simply written off ([THP](https://www.thp.co.uk/what-is-a-suspense-account/)).

## Auditors start at 5% of profit, then let nature override

Audit materiality is the only fully specified method, which is why preparers borrow from it. ISA (UK) 320 requires the auditor to document four things: overall materiality, specific (lower) materiality for sensitive areas, performance materiality, and any revisions. The file must also record "the factors considered in their determination" (paras 10–14) ([ISA (UK) 320](https://media.frc.org.uk/documents/ISA_UK_320_Revised_June_2016_Updated_September_2025.pdf)). The standard contains only one pair of percentages: **5% of profit before tax for a manufacturer and 1% of revenue or expenses for a not-for-profit**, and "higher or lower percentages... may be deemed appropriate" (A8). It gives benchmark choices for the awkward cases that dominate small practice. These are gross profit or revenue when PBT is volatile (A5), and normalised PBT from past results when the year is exceptional (A6). For an owner-managed business whose profit is "consistently nominal" because the owner takes it as pay, it suggests "profit before remuneration and tax" (A9) ([ISA (UK) 320](https://media.frc.org.uk/documents/ISA_UK_320_Revised_June_2016_Updated_September_2025.pdf)).

Firm methodologies supply the remaining numbers. The FRC's 2017 thematic review of the eight largest firms found the following ranges in their guidance ([FRC 2017 thematic review](https://www.frc.org.uk/docs/3355/html/)):

| Parameter | Range in firm guidance | Most common |
|---|---|---|
| Profit before tax | 3–10% | 5% |
| Revenue or expenses | 0.25–5% | 0.5–2% (PIEs), 0.5–3% (non-PIEs) |
| Net assets or equity | 0–10% | n/a |
| Performance materiality (share of overall) | 40–90% | 50–75% |
| Clearly trivial (share of overall) | 0–5% | 5% was the most frequent setting in the 2013 review |

The 2013 review found clearly trivial set at 1% of materiality in 3 audits, 2% in 7 and 5% in 16 ([FRC 2013 thematic review](https://www.frc.org.uk/docs/1113/html/)). The FRC treats reducing performance materiality in a first-year audit as good practice. It also criticised firms that picked the top of their range, or repeatedly added back "exceptional" items, without justifying it for the specific entity ([FRC 2017](https://www.frc.org.uk/docs/3355/html/)). US big-firm manuals converge on the same figures: six of eight "expect, suggest, or require" 5% of pre-tax income, and seven set performance materiality within 50–75% ([Eilifsen & Messier, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2274845)).

Three concepts in ISA (UK) 450 carry over well to bookkeeping. First, **"clearly trivial" is not another word for "not material"**. It means items "of a wholly different (smaller) order of magnitude, or of a wholly different nature", and "when there is any uncertainty... the misstatement is considered not to be clearly trivial" (A2). An item below the trivial amount is still logged if it is not trivial "when judged on criteria of nature or circumstances" (A3) ([ISA (UK) 450](https://media.frc.org.uk/documents/ISA_UK_450_Revised_June_2016_Updated_September_2025.pdf)). Second, nature can override size. A21 lists circumstances that make a below-materiality item material. These include regulatory compliance, covenants, "increasing management compensation" and items involving parties related to management, and A22 adds possible fraud "even if the size of the misstatement is not material". ISA 320 A11 names related party transactions and "the remuneration of management and those charged with governance" as candidates for lower, specific materiality ([ISA (UK) 320](https://media.frc.org.uk/documents/ISA_UK_320_Revised_June_2016_Updated_September_2025.pdf)). Third, small items add up. Performance materiality exists because "the aggregate of individually immaterial misstatements may cause the financial statements to be materially misstated" (ISA 320 A13). Several immaterial errors in one account may require that account's risk to be reassessed (ISA 450 footnote 13). A large number of small items may be reported as "the number and overall monetary effect" rather than one by one (ISA 450 A27) ([ISA (UK) 450](https://media.frc.org.uk/documents/ISA_UK_450_Revised_June_2016_Updated_September_2025.pdf)).

Non-audit work borrows the concept, not the procedure. ISRS 4410 shares the definition and the size-or-nature test, but has no performance materiality and no clearly trivial floor. Audit software such as CaseWare automates the benchmark-times-percentage calculation for audits ([CaseWare docs](https://documentation.caseware.com/latest/Audit/en_us/Content/User_Planning/Materiality/t_Documenting_materiality.htm)). No evidence was found that UK bookkeepers or accounts preparers formally adopt ISA percentages. The nearest numeric thresholds in bookkeeping, such as Sage 200's small-balance write-off (default maximum 200 in base currency) and HMRC's VAT error-correction limits (net errors up to £10,000, or up to £50,000 if within 1% of box 6), are unrelated to audit materiality. The VAT limits govern how an error is corrected, not whether an item is coded correctly ([Sage 200 help](https://desktophelp.sage.co.uk/sage200/sage200standard/content/Concepts/HKeepWriteOffSmallValues.htm); [VAT Notice 700/45](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/how-to-correct-vat-errors-and-make-adjustments-or-claims-vat-notice-70045)). The FRC's June 2026 SME sandbox adds two points. The users of an owner-managed company may be "limited to owner-managers and providers of finance", so materiality may be higher where ownership is concentrated and there is no external finance, and lower near a covenant breach. And the 2017 ranges "have become a standard, from which practitioners may not have confidence to deviate", although they were derived from larger entities. ICAEW guidance on SME materiality is expected later in 2026 ([FRC SME sandbox](https://www.frc.org.uk/library/innovation-and-improvement-hub/audit-assurance-sandbox/determining-materiality-for-small-and-medium-sized-entities-smes/)).

To show what derived thresholds look like, the table below applies the common ISA conventions to three illustrative clients: 5% of the chosen benchmark, performance materiality at 50–75%, and clearly trivial at 3–5% of overall. These are my own calculations, not published figures.

| Illustrative client | Benchmark chosen | Overall (5%) | Performance (50–75%) | Clearly trivial (3–5%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole trader, profit £30k | Profit | £1,500 | £750–£1,125 | £45–£75 |
| Owner-managed Ltd, nominal PBT, £60k profit before director pay | Profit before remuneration (A9) | £3,000 | £1,500–£2,250 | £90–£150 |
| Ltd, turnover £5m, PBT £400k | PBT | £20,000 | £10,000–£15,000 | £600–£1,000 |

For the smallest client, the trivial floor lands close to the firm's current hard-coded meal limits. For the largest, it is more than ten times higher. That gap is the case against a single fixed limit.

## HMRC rules hinge on facts a receipt rarely shows

Tax returns have no materiality concept. A small misposting can produce an incorrect return, so the HMRC rules below make items "material by nature" in the ISA 450 sense of "regulatory compliance". The key design point is that **the deciding variable in most of these rules is who, what or why, not how much**.

| Item | HMRC rule | Fact the app must hold | Why a £ limit fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meal or hospitality (VAT) | Input VAT on entertaining non-employees is blocked. Staff-only entertainment is recoverable. VAT on events for directors or partners only is not input tax at all. Mixed events are apportioned per head. Overseas customers are potentially recoverable, but a restaurant meal "is very likely to lead to a private use charge" ([Notice 700/65](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/business-entertainment-and-vat-notice-70065)) | Headcount of employees and non-employees, directors-only or not, overseas customer or not, entertainment or subsistence while working away | The block turns on attendees, so a £3 coffee with a client is treated the same as a £3,000 dinner |
| Meal (corporation tax and income tax) | Entertaining is disallowed (CTA 2009 s1298, ITTOIA s45). Staff entertaining stays allowable unless it is "incidental to" entertaining others. An employee taking a customer to a restaurant makes the whole cost disallowable ([BIM45034](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim45034)) | Guests present, and whether the employer would have paid with no guests | The test is about purpose and attendees |
| Staff party | Exempt only if the cost per head of annual function(s) is £150 or less, the event recurs yearly and it is open to staff generally. "The figure of £150 is not an allowance": exceed it and the full cost is taxed ([EIM21690](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/employment-income-manual/eim21690)) | Cost per head across all attendees (VAT and transport included), cumulative across the year, whether annual and open to all | It is a cliff, computed per head and per year, not per receipt |
| Small staff gift | Trivial benefit: £50 or less, not cash or a cash voucher, not contractual, not a reward for services; £300 a year cap for directors of close companies ([EIM21864](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/employment-income-manual/eim21864)) | Recipient, form of the gift, reason, director status, cumulative total | The four conditions are factual; the £300 is an annual aggregate |
| Client gift | Disallowed unless it carries a conspicuous advertisement, is not food, drink, tobacco or a voucher, and costs £50 or less per recipient per period ([BIM45070](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim45070)). VAT has a separate £50-per-person rule ([VATSC03322](https://gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/vat-supply-and-consideration/vatsc03322)) | Recipient, contents, logo, cumulative amount to that recipient | A £20 bottle of wine fails at any price |
| VAT reclaim | A simplified invoice is allowed only up to £250 including VAT ([Notice 700/21](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/record-keeping-for-vat-notice-70021)). Alternative evidence is accepted "only in exceptional circumstances" ([VIT31200](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/vat-input-tax/vit31200)) | Supplier VAT number, VAT shown, full invoice above £250 | Without a valid invoice the VAT is lost at any amount |
| Subsistence | Benchmark rates are £5 (5+ hours), £10 (10+ hours) and £25 (15+ hours and travelling at 8pm), for qualifying travel only ([EIM30240](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/employment-income-manual/eim30240)) | Journey, duration, temporary or permanent workplace | Eligibility depends on travel, not on the meal price |
| Mileage | AMAP for cars and vans is 55p for the first 10,000 business miles from 6 April 2026 (45p before), then 25p ([GOV.UK rates](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/rates-and-allowances-travel-mileage-and-fuel-allowances/travel-mileage-and-fuel-rates-and-allowances)) | Business miles, vehicle type, tax year, cumulative miles | The rate depends on the year and the running total |
| Director or personal spend | A director's loan account debit attracts s455 tax at 35.75% on loans made from 6 April 2026 (33.75% before) ([Crowe](https://www.crowe.com/uk/insights/budget-2025-dividend-tax-rate)) | Whether the cost was for the director's personal benefit, and when | A related-party and tax item at any size |
| Small equipment | No monetary de minimis: whether spend is capital depends on its nature and effect, not its cost ([BIM35000](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim35000)). AIA stays at £1m, a 40% first-year allowance applies from 1 January 2026, and the main pool WDA falls to 14% from April 2026 ([Budget 2025 OOTLAR](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2025-overview-of-tax-legislation-and-rates-ootlar/budget-2025-overview-of-tax-legislation-and-rates-ootlar)) | What the item is, whether it lasts more than a year, entity type, whether it is a car, the client's policy | The tax effect is usually nil either way, so the question is about presentation, not tax |
| Prepayments | Needed only under GAAP or accruals. The cash basis is the default for unincorporated businesses from 2024–25 ([BIM72001](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim72001)). HMRC accepts "any reasonable method... consistently applied" ([BIM42215](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim42215)) | Accounting basis, service period start and end | For cash-basis clients the question should not exist |

Two patterns stand out from the table. First, the real £ thresholds in tax law (£50 trivial benefits, £50 client gifts, £150 annual functions, £250 simplified invoices) are **conditions inside a fact-based rule**. They are usually measured per head, per recipient or per year, never per receipt line. A per-receipt "£50 meal" limit matches none of them. Second, two of the firm's current questions often cannot change anything. Expensing a small capital item for a company is normally tax-neutral because of the AIA, and a prepayment question is meaningless for a cash-basis sole trader. The safe default for a meal with no attendee information is to treat it as entertainment and not reclaim the VAT. Under that default a question is worth asking only if the recoverable VAT and tax relief at stake are worth the client's time ([Notice 700/65](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/business-entertainment-and-vat-notice-70065)). A few details in the HMRC notes remain unverified: the exact article of the VAT (Input Tax) Order 1992 behind the entertainment block, the 2026 notice's wording on a host's own meal, and whether full expensing is still in force.

## Software ships the rule slots and leaves the numbers blank

Across expense and receipt tools, **the vendor defines the types of rule and the customer supplies the amounts**. Expensify offers "flag expenses above amount", receipts or itemised receipts above set amounts, and per-category requirements for attendees and descriptions, with no default figures published. Category rules beat general rules, and per-attendee limits work from cost per head ([Expensify Workspace Rules](https://help.expensify.com/articles/new-expensify/workspaces/Workspace-Rules)). Ramp's Policy Agent reads the customer's own written policy, converted into a structured document ([Ramp Policy Agent](https://support.ramp.com/hc/en-us/articles/44072387128979-Policy-Agent-Overview)). Dext auto-publish is set per supplier, per payment method or globally, with a set precedence order. It has fixed blockers: missing essential fields, suspicious items, and document types that never auto-publish ([Dext auto-publish](https://help.dext.com/en/articles/377051-how-to-use-auto-publish-in-dext)). Brex prompts for attendees on Meals only if an admin turns that on ([Brex Assistant](https://brex.com/support/brex-assistant)). The only numeric default found anywhere is Expensify's random audit of *compliant* reports, which sends 5% of them to a human ([Expensify random audit](https://help.expensify.com/articles/expensify-classic/reports/Set-a-random-report-audit-schedule)).

Ledgers and AI bookkeepers use model confidence to decide what posts automatically, and **low confidence routes to the accountant, not the client**. Xero JAX auto-reconciles only at "high confidence", claims over 60% of lines reconciled at about 96% accuracy (measured as lines users did not later correct), and lets each bank account be toggled ([Xero campaign page](https://www.xero.com/id/campaign/auto-bank-reconciliation/); [Xero Blog](https://blog.xero.com/product-updates/automatic-bank-reconciliation-jax-beta/)). QuickBooks shows green and blue badges and hides low-confidence suggestions. In a new company file, even a clean match may not show green until the user has accepted that match type several times, which is a built-in cold-start safeguard ([QuickBooks Help](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/learn-support/en-global/help-article/bank-transactions/ai-suggestions-help-match-categorise-bank/L8FHOh4AD_ROW_en)). Docyt defaults to sending anything under 90% confidence to "Accountant Review" and lets firms raise or lower that ([Docyt](https://www.docyt.com/article/how-to-trust-your-ai-without-losing-your-mind/)). A search excerpt of Botkeeper's knowledge base shows three bands: 98%+, 90–97.9% and below 90% ([Botkeeper KB](https://botkeeper.com/en/knowledge/confidence-rating)). Ramp, Pleo and Concur Detect use three-way verdicts with an explicit "uncertain" bucket. They show the reasons and the cited policy text rather than a numeric score ([Ramp Policy Agent](https://support.ramp.com/hc/en-us/articles/44072387128979-Policy-Agent-Overview)). Accuracy claims of 97–99% are all self-reported. Accounting Today noted that Digits' 98% test used a set with "fewer edge cases and more repeat types" ([Accounting Today](https://www.accountingtoday.com/news/digits-says-its-new-ai-agents-can-automate-95-of-bookkeeping-tasks)).

The weakness of confidence gating is calibration. One bookkeeper reports an AI coding every recurring Home Depot purchase as Office Supplies "with 94% confidence". The model was consistently wrong, so the threshold never fired ([beancount.io forum](https://beancount.io/forum/t/i-adopted-ai-bookkeeping-for-time-savings-but-reviewing-every-transaction-takes-just-as-long/1823)). Accountants counter this with checks against history, risk rules by account and sampling. Practitioners advise always reviewing fixed assets, owner draws and intercompany items, and spot-checking 10–15% of items below the threshold each month ([beancount.io forum](https://beancount.io/forum/t/ai-bookkeeping-tools-promise-98-accuracy-but-the-2-errors-compound-into-tax-penalties-how-do-you-validate-ai-categorization-at-scale/4031); [beancount.io controls guide](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/08/31/ai-finance-automation-controls-approval-limits-source-documents-audit-trail)). Gartner warns that materiality thresholds "can hide small leakages and breaches that accumulate over time" ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/8238493)).

Client-query tools are a separate layer and always batch. Digits pre-fills a question for each transaction, but nothing goes out until the accountant clicks "Review & Send". Reminders are set per client, and confirmed answers are written back to QBO with the Q&A attached ([Digits Transaction Review](https://digits.com/blog/introducing-digits-transaction-review/)). Double sends clients "a single email each morning with all their unanswered questions" ([Keeper changelog](https://keeperapp.canny.io/changelog/august-21-2025)). In one vendor case study it reports a 90% response rate within a week of launch ([Double case study](https://www.casestudies.com/company/doublehq/case-study/489c-rei-bookkeepers-gets-15k-client-questions-answered-per-month-using-doubles-client-portal)). Client Hub's queue mixes large transactions, missing payees and missing attachments, with no documented threshold ([Client Hub](https://apps.xero.com/uk/function/accountant-tools-extras/app/client-hub)). **No product found documents a "don't ask below £x" setting, a materiality derived from client size, or a queue ranked by money at stake.** Concur Detect's prioritisation "based on confidence and potential impact" is the nearest, and it sits at the enterprise end ([Concur Detect](https://www.concur.com.au/detect-expense-fraud)). Acting Office's AccaBot publishes no confidence or escalation mechanics ([AccountingWEB sponsored](https://www.accountingweb.co.uk/community/industry-insights/acting-office-is-transforming-accounting-and-bookkeeping-with-accabot)).

Learning from corrections follows one pattern: explicit rules first, learned memory second, and memory promoted to rules only with approval. Xero applies bank rules before memory and prediction, and memory can be switched off ([Xero Product Ideas](https://productideas.xero.com/forums/967121-users-setup/suggestions/49510907-bank-reconciliation-add-setting-to-disable-sugg)). Dext suggests suppliers that are "ready for auto-publish" based on items published without manual edits. Dext AI Assist proposes rules across the client base, each with a plain-English explanation, but none applies "until your expertise says so" ([Dext blog](https://dext.com/au/blog/single/do-more-with-dext-guide-to-everyday-automation-wins); [Dext AI Assist](https://dext.com/uk/partner/product/dext-ai-assist-bookkeeping-agent)). Dismissing a Dext Precision duplicate flag stops that match being raised again ([Dext Precision](https://help.dext.com/en/articles/276847-using-the-duplicate-transactions-check)).

## Replace each fixed limit with a derived, overridable policy

The research supports a decision function with three outcomes: code it, send it to the accountant, or ask the client. Each should be decided separately. Confidence decides between coding and accountant review. Asking the client should depend on three conditions holding together: a fact the app cannot infer is missing, that fact would change the VAT, tax or accounts treatment, and either the money at stake clears the client's own materiality or a nature flag says ask regardless. That framing comes from my reading of ISRS 4410 paras 31–32 and ISA 450 A2/A3/A21, not from any single published design. One further point is my inference. Defaults that can only overpay tax are safer to leave unasked than defaults that can underpay it. Treating an unexplained meal as entertainment with no VAT reclaimed costs the client money. Leaving an unidentified receipt in sundry or coding personal spend as business understates tax, which is the error HMRC targets ([ICAEW](https://www.icaew.com/insights/tax-news/2025/aug-2025/hmrc-targets-personal-expenditure); [THP](https://www.thp.co.uk/what-is-a-suspense-account/)). Questions should be weighted toward the second kind.

| Current fixed rule | What the industry actually does | Proposed replacement |
|---|---|---|
| £20 limit for a meal on an expense claim; £50 for other meals | No meal limit exists in HMRC rules; VAT and corporation tax turn on attendees and purpose. Expense tools require attendees by category and compute cost per head | Take attendees from the claim note or receipt where possible. With no information, default to entertainment and no VAT reclaim. Ask only if the VAT plus tax relief at stake exceeds the client's trivial level, or a nature flag applies (directors only, possible personal spend, possible overseas customer). Track £150-per-head functions and £50 gifts per recipient and per year |
| £100/£500 capitalisation | Per-client policy, commonly £250–£1,000, recorded on file and used for tax as well. Expensing is usually tax-neutral under the AIA | Firm-set capitalisation policy per client, defaulted from the client's size and editable, applied consistently. Always flag cars (excluded from AIA and the cash-basis deduction) and items far above the policy. Ask only when what the item is cannot be inferred |
| £300 prepayment | Per-client floor, about £100–£1,000. Irrelevant on the cash basis | Skip entirely for cash-basis clients. For GAAP clients, use a firm-set floor, and post only recurring identifiable period items (insurance, rent, subscriptions) using the invoice's service dates |
| Always ask about Sundry | Small unknown items are tolerated in sundry below a client-specific level where no VAT is reclaimed. Suspense is cleared by year end. Unidentified receipts of money are always queried | Auto-accept to sundry only when the item is money out, below clearly trivial, has no VAT reclaimed and no nature flag, and the running sundry total stays below a share of performance materiality. Always query unidentified money in. Escalate when sundry or suspense totals build up |

The materiality engine behind these defaults should copy the audit method's structure and its documentation duty, while labelling the result as firm policy. For benchmark selection, it should use PBT when profit is positive and stable, and profit before director remuneration when PBT is nominal (A9). It should fall back to revenue at 0.5–2% or total expenses at about 1% when the client is loss-making or profit is volatile, use total assets for property or asset-heavy clients, and use a normalised figure when the current year is exceptional. It should record the benchmark and the reason, as ISA 320 para 14 requires. From that figure it derives overall, performance (50–75%) and clearly trivial (3–5%, never above 5%) levels. The firm can override any of them, with the most specific setting winning, as in Expensify and Dext. A new client with no history should start at the prudent end. That means the bottom of the performance range, as the FRC recommends for first-year audits, and asking more until the firm's acceptances build a track record, as QuickBooks does. The firm should be able to tell the engine that a client's users are only the owner (higher materiality) or that there is a lender with covenants (lower), following the FRC sandbox ([FRC SME sandbox](https://www.frc.org.uk/library/innovation-and-improvement-hub/audit-assurance-sandbox/determining-materiality-for-small-and-medium-sized-entities-smes/)).

Nature flags must override size, and "any uncertainty means not trivial" should be the rule the code enforces. Flags should cover directors or related parties, VAT recovery that depends on unknown facts, entertainment or gifts, possible personal spend, unidentified income, a correction to a period already filed for VAT, a possible duplicate, and an amount far outside the supplier's own history. Small items need an aggregate view. The app should log every item it auto-cleared or coded on a default, total them by account against performance materiality, escalate an account when several small corrections land in it (ISA 450 footnote 13), and offer the accountant a count and a total for the remaining small items (A27). It should also send a random sample of auto-coded items to review, following Expensify's 5% and practitioners' 10–15%, because confident systematic errors slip past every confidence gate. Client questions should go out as one batched, per-client digest with a proposed answer on each line, and the firm should approve the list before it is sent. Corrections should turn into explicit, explained rules that the firm approves, never into silent drift. Reviewers should see the reason for each decision, and not only a score.

## Conclusion

The industry's "threshold" is really two separate mechanisms that the app currently folds into one number. One is an accounts materiality that scales with the client and decides presentation questions: capitalise or expense, post a prepayment or not, leave an item in sundry or clear it. The other is a set of fact-based tax rules that ignore size and decide what has to be known: who ate, who received the gift, whether there is a valid VAT invoice, whether a cost is the director's. Fixed limits such as £20 or £50 for a meal fail both tests. They are too coarse for the first mechanism and measure the wrong variable for the second. Separating the two, and asking only when an unknown fact would change an outcome that matters for that client, is both more faithful to UK practice and likely to cut the number of client questions.

There is also a product opening. No mainstream or AI-first tool documents materiality worked out per client, impact-ranked review, or "don't ask below £x" logic. Each of those is grounded in published standards (ISA 320/450, ISRS 4410) and HMRC manuals that a UK firm can defend at a quality review. Two things could shift the design: ICAEW's SME materiality guidance, expected later in 2026, and mandatory B2B e-invoicing from April 2029, which changes the VAT invoice checks ([GOV.UK e-invoicing roadmap](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/e-invoicing-implementation-roadmap/roadmap-for-e-invoicing-implementation)). The TECH 07/16 AAF wording on compilation enquiries is still unread and is worth obtaining through an ICAEW login before the policy defaults are finalised.
